Spatial Data Lab

Most of the trouble with spatial data in economics comes from five places. None of them announces itself. Your software runs, a column appears, and the number in it is wrong.

So rather than warn you about them, this page lets you cause each one and watch what it does. Drag something, then read the numbers under the picture. That is where the lesson usually is.

How coarse a raster can get before the average stops meaning anything

Below is a night-lights image with one district boundary drawn over it. Zonal statistics works by reading every cell whose centre falls inside that boundary and returning a single summary number. Coarsen the cells and you are not just losing detail in the picture; you are losing the observations the average is built from.

Worth doing

  1. Drag to 16 km and count what is left inside the boundary.
  2. Compare the mean at 1 km and at 16 km, then compare the standard errors beside them. One of those means belongs in a regression and the other does not.
  3. Watch the sum while you drag. It swings hard; the mean barely moves. That is the whole argument for using rates rather than totals.

In QGIS this is Processing Toolbox › Zonal statistics. Tick count as well as mean, always. A small city district can hold a dozen cells, and nobody downstream will know.

Why 5000 sometimes means five kilometres and sometimes five hundred

Three villages, twelve health facilities. You want to know how many facilities lie within five kilometres of each village, so you type 5000 into the distance box. Whether that is five kilometres or roughly five hundred depends entirely on which coordinate system the layer happens to carry.

Worth doing

  1. Leave the value at 5 and switch to degrees. Read the radius you actually got.
  2. Still in degrees, look for a value that gives a sensible answer. There isn't one.
  3. Back in metres, find the smallest value that gives every village at least one facility.

A degree is an angle. One degree of latitude runs about 111 km, and a degree of longitude shrinks as you move away from the equator, so the error is not even constant across your map. Run Reproject Layer into the right UTM zone before anything that measures, and glance at the EPSG code in the bottom-right corner of the window before you press Run.

One column of numbers, and how different the maps can look

Fifty-four areas, one poverty figure each. That figure does not change anywhere on this page. What changes is where the range gets cut into classes and which colours those classes are given — two decisions the data does not make for you, and which most readers will never think about.

Worth doing

  1. Switch to Quantile and look at the histogram. The four break lines pile up where the data is dense, which is how a map of nearly identical values ends up looking dramatic.
  2. Change the variable to the number of poor people. Ask yourself whose map that is.
  3. Pick Rainbow and try to rank the classes from low to high using colour alone.
  4. Watch the width of the top class as you change method. Equal Interval and Quantile are answering different questions.

All of this sits in Layer Properties › Symbology › Graduated. Name the method and the class count in your figure caption; without them nobody can judge the map, and a referee will ask.

The join that drops a third of your sample without saying so

Twelve districts on one side, twelve rows of statistics on the other. Switch on any of the ordinary conditions below and watch the match rate. Then look at which rows fell out, because that matters more than how many.

Worth doing

  1. Switch on the first condition alone and see how many rows survive.
  2. Turn it off, switch on the third, and read which names fail. Each failure makes sense once you see it.
  3. Switch on the second and third together, then read the figure for 514 districts.

The rows that fail are never a random draw. New districts, renamed cities and merged areas differ systematically from the ones that match, which turns a data-cleaning annoyance into a sample selection problem. Count your rows before and after every join, sort the new column to find the blanks, and join on codes rather than names. A join in Layer Properties › Joins is temporary until you run Export › Save Features As.

What happens to a coefficient when you redraw the boundaries

Four hundred households, and one relationship between road access and spending that stays exactly the same throughout. Average them into larger and larger areas and watch the slope. Nothing new enters the data. The grey dashed line is the household-level relationship, kept for comparison.

Worth doing

  1. Note the coefficient at household level, then at four areas, and work out the multiple.
  2. At sixteen areas, switch the boundary shift on and off. Same households, different lines on the map, different answer.
  3. Watch R squared climb as you aggregate. A tighter fit is not a truer one.

Averaging within an area cancels the noise that belongs to each household but keeps whatever varies smoothly across space, so what survives is the spatial part of the relationship. Here that part is much stronger than the household one, which is why the slope grows. The first effect is about scale, the second about where the lines are drawn; together they are the modifiable areal unit problem, and there is no general fix for it. Choose the unit from a mechanism you can defend, then report a second unit as a robustness check.
