Spatial Data Lab

Spatial data goes wrong in five ordinary ways. None of them shows an error. QGIS runs, a new column appears, and the number inside it is simply not what you think it is. This page lets you make each mistake on purpose and watch the figures underneath change while you do.

How coarse a raster can get before the average stops meaning anything

Below is a satellite image of night lights. Every square is one cell, and every cell holds one number: how bright that patch of ground was. The pink outline is a district. QGIS can average all the cells inside that outline and hand you one figure for the district. That tool is called zonal statistics. Make the cells bigger and fewer of them fit inside the outline, so the average has less to stand on.

Worth doing

  1. Drag to 16 km and count what is left inside the boundary.
  2. Compare the mean at 1 km and at 16 km, then compare the standard errors beside them. One of those means belongs in a regression and the other does not.
  3. Watch the sum while you drag. It swings hard; the mean barely moves. That is the whole argument for using rates rather than totals.

In QGIS you will find this under Processing Toolbox › Zonal statistics. When you run it, tick count as well as mean. Count tells you how many cells went into each average. A small city district might contain only a dozen, and without that column nobody reading your table would ever know.

Why 5000 sometimes means five kilometres and sometimes five hundred

Three villages, twelve health facilities. You want to count the facilities within five kilometres of each village, so you draw a circle of that size around every village. In QGIS that circle is called a buffer, and you set its size by typing a number. Type 5000 and you might get five kilometres, or you might get five hundred. Which one you get depends on the coordinate system the layer carries, and QGIS will not stop you either way.

Leave the value at 5 and switch the system to degrees. Then try to find any value that still gives a sensible answer, because there isn't one.

A coordinate system stores position either in metres or in degrees of latitude and longitude. A degree is an angle, not a length. One degree of latitude covers about 111 km, so asking for a buffer of 5 in a degree-based layer gives you a circle roughly 555 km across. Worse, a degree of longitude gets shorter as you move away from the equator, so the mistake is not even the same size across your map. And once every village reaches every facility, your new column is the same number everywhere. A column with no variation tells a regression nothing at all.

The fix is one step. Before anything that measures distance or area, run Reproject Layer and send your data into the UTM zone that covers your study area, which puts it in metres. Then check the EPSG code in the bottom-right corner of the QGIS window before you press Run.

One column of numbers, and how different the maps can look

Fifty-four areas, each with one poverty figure. Those figures never change while you are on this page. What changes is how you slice them. To colour a map you first sort the values into groups, called classes, and then give each class a shade. Where you cut between one class and the next is your choice, not the data's, and so is the colour scheme. Most readers will never think about either.

Three things to try

  1. Switch to Quantile and watch where the break lines land on the histogram. They pile up where the data is dense, which is how a map of nearly identical values ends up looking dramatic.
  2. Change the variable to the number of poor people, then ask whose map that actually is.
  3. Pick Rainbow and try to rank the classes from low to high using colour alone.

Every control here has a twin in QGIS, under Layer Properties › Symbology › Graduated. When you put a map in a paper, write the method and the number of classes into the caption. Without them a reader cannot tell whether the pattern is in the data or in your choices, and a referee will ask.

The join that drops part of your sample without saying so

A join is how you attach a table of statistics to a map. Both sides need a shared key, usually the district code, and QGIS matches them row by row. Here are twelve districts on the map and twelve rows of statistics. Switch on any of the everyday situations below and watch how many rows still match. Then look at which ones dropped out, because that matters far more than the count.

The rows that fail are never a random handful. They are the newly created districts, the cities whose names changed, the areas that merged. Those places are different from the ones that matched, often in ways related to what you are studying. So this is not a tidying-up problem. It is a sample selection problem sitting quietly inside your results.

So count your rows before and after every join, sort the new column to find the blanks, and match on codes rather than names. A join made in Layer Properties › Joins is temporary until you run Export › Save Features As.

What happens to a coefficient when you redraw the boundaries

Four hundred households. The true relationship between road access and spending is fixed and never changes here. Now group those households into areas and average each area, which is what you do whenever you work with district data instead of household data. Watch the slope of the line as the areas get bigger. No new information arrives; the grey dashed line is the household relationship, kept for comparison.

At sixteen areas, switch the boundary shift on and off a few times. Same households, different lines on the map, different answer.

Averaging cancels out whatever is peculiar to each household, but it keeps whatever varies smoothly from place to place. So what survives the averaging is the geographic part of the relationship, and here that part is much stronger than the household part. That is why the slope grows. R squared climbs too, which is worth noticing: a tighter fit is not a truer answer.

Two separate things are happening. Bigger areas change the answer, and moving the boundaries changes it again even when the number of areas stays the same. Together they are known as the modifiable areal unit problem, and there is no general cure for it. Pick your unit because you can explain why that is the right one, then show the result at a second unit so readers can see how much it moved.
