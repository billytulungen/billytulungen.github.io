Module 01

Raster resolution and zonal statistics

A satellite image of night lights sitting under one district boundary. Make the cells coarser and two things happen at once: the coastline turns into a staircase, and the number of cells inside the boundary collapses until the average stops meaning very much.

At this resolution the average rests on plenty of cells.

Controls

Try this
  1. Drag to 16 km cells. How many cells are left inside the boundary?
  2. Compare the mean at 1 km and at 16 km. Now compare the standard errors beside them. Which mean would you put in a regression?
  3. Watch the sum as you drag. It swings wildly, while the mean holds much steadier. That is why rates travel better than totals.

What this shows

Zonal statistics reads every raster cell whose centre falls inside a polygon and returns one summary number for that polygon. The coarser the raster, the fewer cells there are to summarise.

What to notice

  • The mean barely moves; the sum moves a lot. Totals depend on how many cells you happen to have.
  • The standard error grows as cells disappear. Same district, same underlying light, far less precision.
  • The boundary itself does not change. Only your ability to measure inside it does.

In QGIS

The same operation, with the same trap:

Processing Toolbox › Zonal statistics

Always tick count alongside mean. A small city district can hold a dozen cells, and its average will jump around for no economic reason.

Module 02

One number, two units

Three villages and twelve health facilities. You want to count the facilities within 5 km of each village, so you type 5000 into the Distance box. Pick the wrong coordinate system and that number means something else entirely — with no warning of any kind.

metres

Actual radius

5 km

Per village

0 · 0 · 0

Pairs counted

0

This looks sensible: some facilities fall outside the reach of each village.

Controls

Try this
  1. Leave the value at 5 and switch the system to degrees. What radius do you actually get?
  2. In degrees, is there any value at all that still gives a sensible answer?
  3. Back in metres, find the smallest value that gives every village at least one facility.

What this shows

A buffer is drawn in whatever units the layer carries. Degrees are an angle, not a length: one degree of latitude is roughly 111 km. So 5 in a degree-based layer is about 555 km, not 5 km.

What to notice

  • Nothing errors. The tool runs, a new column appears, and it is wrong by a factor of a hundred.
  • In degree mode every village counts every facility, so the variable has no variation left — and a variable with no variation quietly kills your regression.
  • A degree of longitude also shrinks as you move away from the equator, so the error is not even constant across your map.

In QGIS

Reproject first, every time:

Reproject Layer › Buffer › Join Attributes by Location

Check the EPSG code in the bottom-right corner of the window before you press Run. For Indonesia, pick the UTM zone your study area sits in.

Same data, different maps

Fifty-four areas with one poverty number each. That number never changes while you are on this page. All that changes is how the range is cut into classes and which colours those classes get. Watch how far the impression can shift.

Controls

Try this
  1. Switch to Quantile and look at the histogram. See where the four break lines pile up.
  2. Change the variable to the number of poor people. Whose map are you really looking at now?
  3. Pick Rainbow, then try to rank the classes from low to high using colour alone.
  4. Compare "width of top class" across methods. Equal Interval and Quantile answer very different questions.

What this shows

A choropleth has two separate decisions inside it: where to cut the range into classes, and which colours to assign. Neither is given by the data, and both change what a reader concludes.

What to notice

  • Quantile always puts the same number of areas in each class, so a map of nearly identical values still looks dramatic.
  • Equal Interval on skewed data often leaves the top class almost empty — one or two outliers carrying a whole colour.
  • Mapping counts instead of rates reproduces the population distribution, whatever the title says.

In QGIS

Every control here has a twin in the dialog:

Layer Properties › Symbology › Graduated

State the method and the number of classes in your figure caption. Without them a reader cannot judge the map, and a referee will ask.

The join that fails without telling you

Twelve districts in the polygon layer, twelve rows in the statistics table. Switch on one ordinary real-world condition and watch how many rows still match — and notice that nothing anywhere reports a problem.

Everything matches. This is what a healthy join looks like.

Conditions in force

Try this
  1. Switch on the first one alone. How many rows survive?
  2. Turn it off and switch on the third. Which names fail, and why does each failure make sense?
  3. Switch on the second and third together, then read the "equivalent on 514" figure.
  4. Look at which rows drop, not just how many. Are they a random selection of districts?

What this shows

A join matches rows on a key. When the key does not line up, the unmatched rows are not deleted and not flagged — they simply carry empty columns into everything you do next.

What to notice

  • The rows that fail are never random. New districts, renamed cities and merged areas are systematically different from the ones that match.
  • That makes it a sample selection problem, not a data-cleaning annoyance.
  • A type mismatch kills every row at once, which is oddly the safest failure: you notice immediately.

In QGIS

The join itself lives here:

Layer Properties › Joins

Count rows before and after, sort the new column to find empty cells, and join on codes rather than names. A join is temporary until you run Export › Save Features As.

Module 05

The coefficient that moves when you change the unit

Four hundred households with one relationship that never changes: road access against spending. Average them up into larger and larger areas and watch the slope of the regression line. No new data arrives. The only thing that changes is where the boundaries are drawn.

This is the household-level relationship, the one actually generating the data.

Level of aggregation

Try this
  1. Note β at household level, then at 4 areas. How many times larger has it become?
  2. At 16 areas, switch the boundary shift on and off. Same households, different lines on the map, different β.
  3. Watch R² climb as you aggregate. A tighter fit is not a truer answer.

What this shows

Averaging within areas cancels out the noise that is specific to each household, but keeps whatever varies smoothly across space. What survives is the spatial part of the relationship, which here is much stronger than the household one.

What to notice

  • The scale effect: β grows as the areas get bigger, purely from aggregation.
  • The zoning effect: at a fixed number of areas, moving the boundaries still moves β.
  • Together these are the modifiable areal unit problem. It has no general solution, only honest reporting.

In QGIS

Aggregation is a geoprocessing step:

Dissolve › Join Attributes by Location › Export to CSV

Choose the unit from the economic mechanism you believe in, then report the result at a second unit as a robustness check.
